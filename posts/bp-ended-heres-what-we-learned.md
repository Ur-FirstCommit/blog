A behind-the-scenes look at everything we learned from our first hackathon at *FirstCommit*, **Beginner's Paradise**.

When we first started planning Beginner's Paradise, we had relatively small expectations. We wanted to create something for the community, give beginners a place to learn, experiment, and build, and hopefully bring a few people together.

We thought we'd get around 100 participants and maybe one or two sponsors.

We ended up with **1,400+ participants, 300+ project submissions, and 4+ partners.**

Safe to say, things turned out a little differently than expected.

But the numbers aren't the most important part. Organising Beginner's Paradise taught us a lot about running events, coordinating people, and building something for a community. Here's what we learned.

## 1. Always be prepared for things to get bigger than expected

One of our biggest surprises was the sheer number of participants and submissions.

We initially planned around a much smaller event. As registrations grew, so did the amount of work behind the scenes.

Managing submissions, answering questions, coordinating participants, and making sure everything was running smoothly became significantly more challenging.

Something we learned pretty quickly: **always plan for more people than you expect.**

Even if you think 100 people will sign up, prepare as if 500 might.

## 2. Organisation is everything

If there's one thing we'd take away from Beginner's Paradise, it's this.

Organisation matters. A lot.

We underestimated how much coordination goes into running a hackathon. It's not just about launching a website, opening registrations, and waiting for projects to come in.

You need clear information, accessible rules, organised resources, and processes that everyone understands.

One particularly challenging area was submissions and judging.

We received far more submissions than expected, and quite a few didn't follow the submission requirements. Some projects were missing information, while others didn't meet the rules.

Judging also took longer than anticipated. Coordinating judges and making sure projects were actually reviewed properly was another challenge.

Looking back, we'd establish much clearer submission requirements, organise the judging process earlier, and set more defined expectations for judges.

We'd also seriously consider building our own in-house judging software to make the entire process easier to manage.

## 3. Keep your resources and codes organised

This sounds like a small thing, but it can save an incredible amount of time.

Throughout the event, we had different resources, partnerships, and sponsor benefits to coordinate.

For example, we had domain codes from XYZ, alongside benefits and resources from partners like Linear and DevSwarm.

Trying to find the right code, remember where something was shared, or figure out which resource was intended for whom can become surprisingly time-consuming.

Our lesson? Keep everything documented in one place.

Don't rely on Discord messages, scattered documents, or someone's memory. Create a central place for important information, codes, deadlines, and resources.

## 4. Building a community is just as important as running the event

One of the most rewarding parts of Beginner's Paradise wasn't the submissions or the numbers.

It was the people.

Throughout the event, we built a Discord community with **100+ people joining along the way**.

Seeing people interact, ask questions, share their progress, and become part of something was genuinely one of the best parts of organising the event.

We started Beginner's Paradise because we wanted to give beginners an opportunity to learn. Seeing people actually engage with that idea made the work worthwhile.

An event might have a start date and an end date, but the community you build around it can continue long after it finishes.

## 5. Getting sponsors takes time

We initially expected to have one or two sponsors.

In reality, we emailed **30+ companies** to explore potential partnerships.

Getting companies to support a first-time hackathon isn't always easy. You have to reach out, explain what you're building, communicate the value of supporting your event, and sometimes deal with unanswered emails.

However, we ended up working with 4+ partners.

A particularly useful partnership was with XYZ, which provided domain codes for participants. We also had support from Linear and DevSwarm.

One thing we'd recommend to anyone organising an event is to start sponsor outreach early. Don't leave it until the last minute, and make sure you have a clear idea of what you're asking for.

## 6. Coordination is harder than it looks

Running an event with this many participants requires coordination.

You need people working together, information flowing between different parts of the organisation, and everyone understanding what needs to happen.

Even when everyone has good intentions, things can fall through the cracks if responsibilities aren't clear.

We learned that having a team isn't enough. You also need systems that help that team work together.

Clear responsibilities, documented processes, and regular communication make a significant difference.

## What's next?

Beginner's Paradise was our first hackathon at FirstCommit, and it certainly taught us a lot.

We know there are things we could have done better, and there are plenty of things we'd change if we were starting again.

But that's part of building something for the first time.

We're taking these lessons forward as we continue growing FirstCommit and preparing for what's next.

Our next event, **Build for Earth**, is already on the horizon.

We'll be applying everything we've learned from Beginner's Paradise to make the next experience even better.

And if there's one thing we'd tell someone organising their first hackathon, it's this:

**Have a great idea, but don't underestimate the organisation behind it.**

Because building the event is one thing. Making sure everything works behind the scenes is another.
